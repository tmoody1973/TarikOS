# Telling a neglected email from a newsletter

**Date:** 2026-08-13
**Status:** shipped to production

---

## Decision

Find unanswered email threads by asking Gmail twice — once for what arrived,
once for what Tarik sent — and throw out anything whose sender declared itself
a mass mailing in its own headers.

## Why this came up

Zola had five email tools and every one of them either read mail or wrote a
draft. None of them noticed when somebody wrote to Tarik and never got an
answer. That is the failure that costs something real: an invoice question sits
for three days, a producer waits on a yes, and nobody in the system is watching.

What was at stake if we got it wrong is narrower than it sounds. A tool that
lists unanswered threads is only worth having if he *believes* it. Read out one
thread he already answered, or bury the invoice under fifteen automated database
reports, and he stops listening to that part of the brief within a week. There
is no partial credit — a triage tool is trusted or it is noise.

## The first problem: Gmail hides half the conversation

`in:inbox` never returns your own messages. A message you send is filed under
"sent," not "inbox," so the newest message that an inbox search can see for any
conversation is always one you *received*. Ask only the inbox and a thread Tarik
answered from his phone at a red light still looks abandoned.

**Options:**

1. **Ask the inbox and exclude mail from himself.** One search. Costs nothing.
   Still wrong whenever he replied — which is most of the threads that matter.
2. **Open each candidate thread individually and look inside.** Accurate, and
   costs one extra call to Google per thread, so the price grows with the size
   of his inbox.
3. **Ask twice — inbox and sent — and match the two lists by conversation ID.**
   Two calls per mailbox no matter how much mail there is, and it also reveals
   the reverse case: threads where *he* is the one waiting.

**We chose 3 (joint — the handoff recommended it, and a live check confirmed
it).** The confirmation mattered more than the recommendation. A real call to
Google showed both searches return the same fields, including a conversation ID
and a comparable timestamp — and in that very sample, one conversation appeared
in the inbox results with Tarik's own reply 63 seconds newer. That is precisely
the wrong answer option 1 would have spoken out loud, caught before a line of
the tool was written.

**What we gave up:** two calls per mailbox instead of one, and about ten seconds
per run. Also a real blind spot — the tool only looks back seven days, so a
thread he abandoned three weeks ago is invisible to it.

## The second problem: the first honest run was embarrassing

Wired up and pointed at the real inbox, the tool announced **"18 threads are
waiting on you"** and led with three automated database pipeline reports. The
invoice question was ninth.

There is a mute list in the system for exactly this, but it was empty — so this
was not a hypothetical, it was what he would have heard the next morning. Gmail's
own "Primary" tab was no help either: in this account, Google's "this is a real
person" markers sit on newsletters and robots alike.

**Options:**

1. **Ship it and let him mute the noise as he hears it.** No new code. Puts the
   cleanup work on him, and he only does that work if he keeps listening — which
   is the thing the noise destroys.
2. **Guess at what looks automated** — senders with odd names, subjects in
   capitals, anything that arrives daily. Cheap and endlessly wrong; the cost of
   a bad guess is silently hiding a real person's email.
3. **Use the flags bulk senders already set on themselves.** There is a published
   standard for this: a mass mailing carries headers announcing it is a mailing
   list, or was generated by a machine, or offers an unsubscribe link. A person
   writing to you sets none of them.

**We chose 3 (Claude proposed it, Tarik approved the ship).** The appeal is that
it is not a judgement about the sender — it is the sender's own declaration,
made in a header they wrote. Checked against two days of his actual inbox, it
removed the pipeline reports, the promo blasts, the mailing-list traffic and the
PR pitches, and left every genuine human thread untouched. Nineteen candidates
became ten, now led by the invoice.

**What we gave up:** it misses the mailing list that sets no headers at all —
three of his ten remaining threads are exactly that (a city notification list, a
mayor's office blast, an internal daily rundown). We deliberately did not invent
a rule to catch them, because every rule that would have caught them would also
have caught somebody real. That residue is what the mute list is for, and muting
is Tarik's call rather than the code's.

## What we also chose not to build

Nothing here archives, labels, unsubscribes, or sends. Noticing a neglected
thread and acting on one are separate decisions, and only the first has been
made.

## How we'll know if this was right

- The morning brief's opening paragraph names a specific waiting thread by
  person and subject, and Tarik acts on one within a day of hearing it.
- He is never told a thread is waiting that he has already answered. One such
  mistake is worth investigating; two means the join is wrong.
- The count of threads settles somewhere he finds credible. Persistently above
  ten means the filter is too soft; zero for a week means it is too hard.
